# Sense of Us research design (v0.2)

Generated from `research/framework.yaml` by `sou framework-md`. Do not edit by hand.

Find out how societies define who "we" are, how that definition is made, lived and changed, and whether it shapes a society's ability to cooperate, build state capacity and accumulate knowhow.

**Central question.** How do societies answer who "we" are, and does the answer matter for cooperation and prosperity?

## Two strands

**Comparative strand.** Codes 50 societies on nine dimensions with one codebook, using AI profiles checked by human experts and survey data. It answers "how much" and "what kind", and makes societies comparable. Produces: Scores per dimension, a typology, coded transitions over time, hypothesis tests.

**Ethnography and culture strand.** Studies how the "us" is lived, felt, performed and argued about in everyday life, through ethnography, interviews and works of culture. It answers "how" and "why", and catches what the codes flatten. Produces: Readings of particular societies, sites and works, new categories, mechanisms, challenges to the codes.

How the strands feed each other:

- Readings that contradict a code trigger recoding or a revision of the anchors
- Features found in three or more readings become candidate codebook categories
- Large myth gaps, disagreements between coders and surprising clusters point to where fieldwork is most needed
- Readings explain the mechanisms behind statistical links, such as how schooling or football widens the radius

## Four layers of evidence

| Layer | Shows | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | What the society says about itself | Document analysis by AI and human coders |
| Institutional | What the rules encode | Coding of laws and policies, existing indices |
| Behavioural | What people report and do, in aggregate | Surveys and administrative data |
| Lived | How the "us" is experienced and performed in everyday life and culture | Ethnography, interviews and close reading of cultural works |

## How assessment works

1. **Code.** Each dimension gets a code from narrative and institutional evidence, from AI profiles and from two blind expert raters.
2. **Measure.** Where survey data exists, the same dimension gets a behavioural score from the items listed below.
3. **Compare.** The myth gap is the distance between the narrative and behavioural scores. Large gaps are findings, not errors.
4. **Read.** Readings from the ethnography and culture strand say whether lived evidence supports, complicates or contradicts each code.
5. **Check.** Agreement targets are alpha above 0.7 for repeat runs and 0.6 for models, languages and experts. Dimensions that miss them get new anchors.
6. **Revise.** Contradicting readings, open features and weak agreement feed the codebook revision rules in FRAMEWORK.md section 7.

## Questions by dimension

### Radius

*How many people does the operative 'us' include?*

Questions to ask:

- When people say "we", whom do they include without thinking?
- Which groups inside the state are spoken of as "them"?
- Does solidarity reach citizens in other regions, for example through transfers?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Who "the people" are in the constitution preamble, the anthem and founding speeches | Constitute Project, official anthem texts, speech archives |
| Institutional | Birthright citizenship rules, official languages, fiscal equalisation between regions | GLOBALCIT, national fiscal data |
| Behavioural | National versus ethnic self-identification; how much being born in the country matters; intermarriage across groups | Afrobarometer, ISSP National Identity, Pew Research Center, IPUMS International |
| Lived | Who is invited to weddings and funerals; how "we" is used in daily talk; jokes about other regions; mixed neighbourhoods | Ethnographies, interviews, eHRAF World Cultures, oral history archives |

### Boundary permeability

*How easily can an outsider become part of the 'us', in law and in practice?*

Questions to ask:

- How long and how hard is it for an outsider to become a citizen?
- Are the children of migrants treated as part of "us"?
- Which jobs and roles are reserved for nationals, formally or informally?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | How migrants and newcomers appear in textbooks, speeches and national stories | Textbook collections, speech archives, Manifesto Project |
| Institutional | Residence requirement for naturalisation, dual citizenship, reserved professions | GLOBALCIT, MIPEX, labour codes |
| Behavioural | Acceptance of migrants as neighbours, colleagues and family; naturalisation rates | World Values Survey, European Social Survey, Gallup Migrant Acceptance Index, OECD migration statistics |
| Lived | Experiences of naturalised citizens and the second generation; citizenship ceremonies; migrant literature and film | Interviews, ethnographies of migrant neighbourhoods, novels and films by migrant authors |

### Glue

*What primarily binds the 'us' together?*

Questions to ask:

- What do people say makes someone truly one of "us"?
- Which bond do leaders reach for in a crisis?
- Which bond do schools teach, and which do families pass on?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Symbols, holidays, banknote and coin imagery, anthem themes | Central bank banknote descriptions, official holiday calendars, anthem texts |
| Institutional | Status of religion and language in law, civic education, compulsory service | Religion and State project, language laws, national curricula |
| Behavioural | Survey items on what matters for being truly national, such as birth, language, religion, customs or respecting the laws | ISSP National Identity, Pew Research Center |
| Lived | Rituals, festivals, food, sport and music that people treat as shared property | Fieldwork at festivals and matches, ethnographies, popular culture |

### Myth arc

*Which elements of a foundational myth are present and widely taught?*

Questions to ask:

- Which origin story do schools teach?
- Which past is golden and which wound is chosen?
- Are the heroes shared by all groups, or owned by one?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | History textbooks, museum displays, monuments, commemorative calendars | Georg Eckert Institute textbook collections, museum catalogues, monument registers |
| Institutional | Memory laws, legislated holidays, state funding for commemoration | Legal databases, national budgets |
| Behavioural | Pride in national history; which events and figures people name as most important | ISSP National Identity, national history surveys |
| Lived | How myths are retold in families, films, novels and songs; disputes over monuments and street names | Oral histories, literary canon and school reading lists, film archives, OpenStreetMap street names |

### Orientation

*Is the 'us' defined mainly by a shared project or against an enemy?*

Questions to ask:

- Is the "us" defined by what we build together or by whom we oppose?
- Who is named as "them", and how often?
- Is there a shared project that people can name?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Enemy and project framing in speeches, manifestos and news | Manifesto Project, Media Cloud, GDELT, speech archives |
| Institutional | Conscription, military spending, national development plans | SIPRI Military Expenditure Database, development plan documents |
| Behavioural | Threat perceptions and views of other countries; support for national projects | Pew Research Center global surveys, regional barometers |
| Lived | Villains in popular film and television, sporting rivalries, jokes about neighbours, war memorial practice | Box office and broadcast schedules, fieldwork at matches and commemorations |

### Contestation

*How far do rival narratives of 'us' compete within the society?*

Questions to ask:

- How many stories of "us" compete, and who holds each one?
- Is there a movement for autonomy or secession?
- Do groups attend the same schools, read the same media and celebrate the same days?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Rival histories, minority-language media, regionalist and separatist manifestos | Manifesto Project, minority media archives |
| Institutional | Autonomy arrangements, power-sharing rules, groups excluded from power | Ethnic Power Relations, Regional Authority Index, V-Dem |
| Behavioural | Regional versus national identity (the Moreno question); regionalist vote shares; armed conflict | Regional surveys, election results, UCDP |
| Lived | Parallel festivals and schools, which flags are flown where, everyday life in divided towns | Ethnographies of divided towns, fieldwork, interviews |

### Elite–mass bond

*Do elites and ordinary people share institutions, experiences and mutual regard?*

Questions to ask:

- Do elites use the same schools, hospitals and public services as everyone else?
- Do ordinary people trust experts, officials and scientists?
- Do elites speak the language and share the pastimes of ordinary people?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | How leaders talk about "the people" and "the elite"; populist rhetoric | Global Populism Database, speech archives |
| Institutional | Share of private schooling and private health care; universality of service | OECD PISA, WHO health expenditure data, national statistics |
| Behavioural | Trust in the civil service, science and courts; populist vote share | World Values Survey, Wellcome Global Monitor, PopuList |
| Lived | Where elites live, study and holiday; satire and comedy about elites; encounters across class | Ethnographies of elite schools and neighbourhoods, satirical media, interviews |

### Salience

*How strongly is the 'us' felt and how easily can it be mobilised?*

Questions to ask:

- How often and how strongly is the nation invoked?
- Would people make sacrifices for it?
- Is the nation felt every day or only at ceremonies?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | Frequency of national references in media and books over time | Media Cloud, Google Books Ngram Viewer |
| Institutional | Flag laws, number of national holidays, compulsory service | Legal databases, official holiday calendars |
| Behavioural | National pride, closeness to the country, willingness to fight for it | World Values Survey, ISSP National Identity |
| Lived | Flags in daily life, crowds at national matches, consumption of national brands, the banal nationalism of everyday objects | Fieldwork, photo documentation, ethnographies |

### Nesting

*How does this 'us' relate to sub-national and supranational identities?*

Questions to ask:

- How do local, regional, national and supranational loyalties fit together?
- Do gains for one level feel like losses for another?

| Layer | What to look at | Where the data comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative | References to regional and supranational belonging in official texts and speeches | Constitute Project, speech archives |
| Institutional | Regional autonomy, membership of supranational bodies | Regional Authority Index, treaty records |

## Ethnography and culture strand

Codes say how wide, how open or how contested an "us" is. They cannot say what it feels like to belong, how belonging is taught at a kitchen table or a football stadium, or why a minority laughs at a different joke. This strand keeps room for that kind of knowledge and makes it talk back to the codes.

### What it is for

- **Validate.** Check whether the coded "us" matches the lived one, and flag where it does not
- **Discover.** Find features of belonging the codebook has no category for
- **Explain.** Show the mechanisms through which an "us" is made, kept or lost

### Questions

1. How do people say "we" in everyday speech, and whom does it include in practice?
2. Where and how is the nation performed, in rituals, holidays, ceremonies and sport?
3. What do novels, films, songs and television imagine "us" to be, and who is missing from them?
4. How are founding myths passed on, through family, school, church and media, and who disputes them?
5. What is it like to live on the boundary, as a migrant, minority member, returnee or member of the diaspora?
6. Which jokes, stereotypes and embarrassments do insiders share and hide from outsiders?
7. Which places hold the nation's memory, and how do they change when monuments or street names change?
8. Where do elites and ordinary people meet, and where do they avoid each other?

### Methods

| Method | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Participant observation | Festivals, matches, schools, commemorations, citizenship ceremonies, border crossings |
| Semi-structured interviews | A shared interview guide built on the nine dimensions, so answers can be compared across societies |
| Elicitation | Showing photos, songs, banknotes or film clips and asking what they mean and to whom |
| Close reading | Interpretation of novels, films and songs for who counts as "us" and who is "them" |
| Content and discourse analysis | Systematic coding of larger sets of cultural texts, such as all films in a domestic top ten over a decade |
| Archival and oral history work | How earlier generations described the "us", for the change-over-time questions |
| Digital ethnography | Online communities where belonging is argued about |
| Mapping | Street names, monuments and flags across a city or region |

### How readings are assessed

Readings are not scored from 0 to 4. Each one states, for each dimension it speaks to, whether the evidence supports, complicates or contradicts the current code, or points to something new. Readings are judged on the quality criteria below. A dimension with two or more contradicting readings from different materials is flagged for recoding.

| Stance | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| supports | Lived evidence matches the coded value |
| complicates | Matches in part, or only for some groups, places or times |
| contradicts | Lived evidence points to a different value |
| new | Shows something the codebook has no category for |

Quality criteria:

- Every source is named and checkable; nothing is invented or paraphrased as a quotation
- Claims rest on at least two kinds of material or method
- The researcher states their position, languages and relationship to the society
- Where possible, people from the society read the interpretation and respond
- The reading says whose "us" it describes, and whose it leaves out
- Fieldwork follows the ethics approval and consent rules of the researcher's institution

### Reading template

Copy `research/readings/TEMPLATE.md`. Each reading records:

- **Title.** Short and specific
- **Society and period.** A unit id from data/countries, or a new society or sub-national community
- **Site.** The town, institution, event or body of work studied
- **Materials and methods.** From the lists above
- **Dimensions and stance.** For each dimension the reading speaks to, supports, complicates, contradicts or new
- **Summary.** Three to five sentences
- **Observations.** Thick description of what was seen, heard or read
- **Who is in and who is out.** The "us" and "them" in this material
- **What the codebook misses.** Candidate new categories
- **Sources.** Full references, all checked
- **Position and confidence.** Researcher position and how confident the reading is
